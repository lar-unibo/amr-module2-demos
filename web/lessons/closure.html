The opening question

The fingers are fixed and there is no friction: which grasps prevent any motion of the object? The buttons (a), (b), (c) load the three grasps of the slide. Drag a finger along the boundary to make your own.

The grasp matrix

A contact at c_i, with the normal n_i pointing inside the object, pushes with a force along n_i. On the object this is the wrench [\,n_i;\;(c_i-p_B)\times n_i\,]: one column of the grasp matrix

G=\tilde G H^T\in\R^{3\times n_l}.

In the plane, wrenches have three components [f_x,\,f_y,\,m]. A frictionless contact (PwoF) transmits one of them, a hard finger (HF) two: n_l=n_c or 2n_c columns. Either way the finger can only push: f_n\geq 0.

Statics: carry the load, then squeeze

At rest the fingers balance the external wrench, for example the weight: w+G\lambda=0. There are usually more unknowns than equations (n_l against 3 in the plane), so many contact forces do it:

\lambda=\underbrace{-G^{\#}w}_{\text{carry the load}}+\underbrace{\mathcal N(G)\,\gamma}_{\text{squeeze}}.

The internal forces \mathcal N(G)=\{\lambda : G\lambda=0\} give no net wrench on the object, and \gamma says how hard we squeeze. Squeezing does not move the object, but it raises the normal forces, so more friction is available.

Try: press box: the figure of slide 28, with \mu=0.4. The weight mg (orange) pulls at the centre. -G^{\#}w (dashed) gives each finger mg/2 of friction and no normal force, which is outside the cones. The squeeze (wide) adds normal force until each contact force (solid) reaches its cone: \gamma\geq\frac{mg}{2\mu}=1.25\,mg. Make the box soapy: lower \mu and the squeeze grows (10 mg at 0.05). At \mu=0 these two fingers cannot hold it.

Closure asks for more: can the grasp resist any external wrench? Load (c) and switch the weight on. The grasp is not closed, yet the lower finger holds this weight. A push sideways would not be held.

Form closure (first order)

Form closure: the fingers block every motion of the object by geometry alone, with no friction. The bodies are rigid and the finger joints fixed. G^T\nu_B gives the normal velocities at the contacts; a negative one means penetration. No motion is possible without penetrating a finger:

G^T\nu_B\geq 0\;\Longrightarrow\;\nu_B=0.

Equivalently, the columns of G positively span the wrench space: the origin lies inside their convex hull (right). In the plane this needs n_c\geq n_v+1=4 contacts. The count is necessary, not sufficient.

Try: grasp (a) passes. Set fingers to 3: the origin leaves the hull, and the dashed outlines show a motion that leaves a finger. Back to (a), drag finger 3 to the bottom, next to finger 4: still four contacts, but the object escapes. Grasp (c) fails: the object slides sideways or turns.

Grasp (b): the curvature

Two contacts are not enough for the first-order test, and it fails for (b): sliding up and down and turning keep both normal velocities at zero. But on the hourglass every such motion pushes into a finger, because the waist is narrower than the rest. The object is held at second order, by the curvature the first-order test cannot see.

Try: move the side curvature to 0. The box slides between the fingers. Make it negative (a barrel) and the object escapes.

Force closure

Force closure: with friction, the fingers can balance any external wrench, squeezing as needed. With hard fingers (HF) the contact force can lean inside the friction cone |f_t|\leq\mu f_n; in the plane the cone is exactly the two edges f_n[1,\pm\mu].

\forall\,w\;\;\exists\,\lambda\in\mathcal F:\quad G\lambda=-w

The test: \mathrm{rank}\,G=3, and a squeeze \lambda_{\text{int}}\in\mathcal N(G) lies strictly inside the cones (blue). Then, whatever wrench comes, squeezing harder brings every contact force into its cone. It is the same hull test, applied to the cone edges. Form closure holds by geometry, force closure by squeezing and friction.

Try: pinch holds for any \mu>0, because the line between the fingers is along both normals. In tilted pinch that line is 34.2° away from the normals. It fails until the cone half-angle \arctan\mu is wider, at \mu>0.68. When it fails, the red wrench at the centre is one the fingers cannot balance. With the weight on, just above the limit (\mu=0.69) holding it takes a squeeze of 37 mg; at \mu=1, 1.2 mg. Load (b) or (c) and switch to friction: two hard fingers are enough in the plane. In space the object could spin about the line between them, unless the fingers are soft.

Slides and simulation

Lecture 3, slides 8 and 23–30, in the plane: twists and wrenches have 3 components, so first-order form closure needs 4 contacts (7 in space), and two hard fingers can be force closure (in space: two soft fingers, or three hard ones). Contacts are points; the disks only show where the fingers are. Moments are divided by a length \rho, the mean distance of the boundary from the centre, before the hull is built. This does not change the tests. The margin is the distance of the origin from the boundary of the hull, in these units.

The hull of the primitive wrenches (at most 12 points) is computed exactly, by trying every triple of points as a face. When all first-order free motions are tangential, the object is moved along each of them by up to 0.2 (the object is 2 tall), and the finger clearances g are measured on the true shape. At second order the object may also shift by a small correction a, which uses the room gained at one finger to free the others. A motion escapes if some a gives G^Ta+g\geq 0; the object counts as held if none does, within 2\cdot10^{-4}. This feasibility problem, and the internal force, are solved by non-negative least squares.

With the weight on, the contact forces shown are those with the smallest total normal force, a linear program solved exactly by trying every basis of 3 primitive wrenches. -G^{\#}w is the minimum-norm solution, and the squeeze is the difference, in \mathcal N(G). When \mathcal N(G) has dimension 1, \gamma is the largest normal force of the squeeze; for two opposite fingers it is the normal force of each. Arrows are scaled together, and kept shorter than half the distance between two fingers. Rigid point contacts cannot carry the weight of grasp (b): its normals are horizontal. The curvature holds it only after a small sinking, which needs some compliance.

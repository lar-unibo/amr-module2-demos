The opening question

The fingers are fixed and there is no friction: which grasps prevent any motion of the object? The buttons (a), (b), (c) load the three grasps of the slide. Drag a finger along the boundary to make your own.

The grasp matrix

A contact at c_i, with the normal n_i pointing inside the object, pushes with a force along n_i. On the object this is the wrench [\,n_i;\;(c_i-p_B)\times n_i\,]: one column of the grasp matrix

G=\tilde G H^T\in\R^{3\times n_l},\qquad w+G\lambda=0.

In the plane, wrenches have three components [f_x,\,f_y,\,m]. A frictionless contact (PwoF) transmits one of them, a hard finger (HF) two: n_l=n_c or 2n_c columns.

Form closure (first order)

G^T\nu_B gives the normal velocities at the contacts; a negative one means penetration. The object is immobilized if no motion avoids every finger:

G^T\nu_B\geq 0\;\Longrightarrow\;\nu_B=0.

Equivalently, the columns of G positively span the wrench space: the origin lies inside their convex hull (right). In the plane this needs n_c\geq n_v+1=4 contacts.

Try: grasp (a) passes. Set fingers to 3: the origin leaves the hull, and the dashed outlines show a motion that leaves a finger. Grasp (c) fails: the object slides sideways or turns.

Grasp (b): the curvature

Two contacts are not enough for the first-order test, and it fails for (b): sliding up and down and turning keep both normal velocities at zero. But on the hourglass every such motion pushes into a finger, because the waist is narrower than the rest. The object is held at second order, by the curvature the first-order test cannot see.

Try: move the side curvature to 0. The box slides between the fingers. Make it negative (a barrel) and the object escapes.

Force closure

With friction (HF) the contact force can lean inside the friction cone |f_t|\leq\mu f_n. In the plane the cone is exactly the two edges f_n[1,\pm\mu]. The grasp is force closure if it can balance any external wrench:

\forall\,w\;\;\exists\,\lambda\in\mathcal F:\quad G\lambda=-w,

that is, \mathrm{rank}\,G=3 and an internal force \lambda_{\text{int}}\in\mathcal N(G) lies strictly inside the cones (orange arrows). The test is the same hull test, applied to the cone edges.

Try: pinch holds for any \mu>0, because the line between the fingers is along both normals. In tilted pinch that line is 34.2° away from the normals. It fails until the cone half-angle \arctan\mu is wider, at \mu>0.68. When it fails, the red wrench at the centre is one the fingers cannot balance. Load (b) or (c) and switch to friction: two hard fingers are enough in the plane.

Slides and simulation

Lecture 3, slides 8 and 23–30, in the plane: twists and wrenches have 3 components, so first-order form closure needs 4 contacts (7 in space), and two hard fingers can be force closure (in space: two soft fingers, or three hard ones). Contacts are points; the disks only show where the fingers are. Moments are divided by a length \rho, the mean distance of the boundary from the centre, before the hull is built. This does not change the tests. The margin is the distance of the origin from the boundary of the hull, in these units.

The hull of the primitive wrenches (at most 12 points) is computed exactly, by trying every triple of points as a face. When all first-order free motions are tangential, the object is moved along each of them by up to 0.2 (the object is 2 tall), and the finger clearances g are measured on the true shape. At second order the object may also shift by a small correction a, which uses the room gained at one finger to free the others. A motion escapes if some a gives G^Ta+g\geq 0; the object counts as held if none does, within 2\cdot10^{-4}. This feasibility problem, and the internal force, are solved by non-negative least squares.

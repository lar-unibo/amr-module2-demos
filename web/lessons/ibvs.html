The control law

Four image points give \vs\in\R^8. Stacking their interaction matrices gives \vL\in\R^{8\times6}, and asking for an exponential decrease of the error, \dot{\ve}=-\lambda\ve, gives

\vv_c=-\lambda\,\Lhp\,(\vs-\vs^{*}).

Press Play: the red points move to the blue ones along nearly straight lines in the image. The paths are straight only to first order: \vL\vL^{+} is a projection, so the part of the error that the camera cannot produce is left aside.

Which interaction matrix?

\vL depends on the features and on the depths, which change during the motion. Three common choices:

  • \vL(\vs), at the current features: computed at every frame;
  • \vL(\vs^{*}), at the desired features: constant, computed once;
  • their mean \tfrac12(\vL(\vs)+\vL(\vs^{*})).

From a general start, all three converge, with different camera paths.

Rotations about the optical axis

The ideal motion is a pure rotation. Straight lines in the image ask for something else: the points move toward the image centre and back out, which the camera produces by translating along its axis.

Try: 1 rad about z with \vL(\vs): the camera retreats and comes back (bottom-right plot). With 2.8 rad about z the retreat is several metres. With π rad about z the straight paths all cross the image centre: the camera retreats to infinity.

Then switch to \vL(\vs^{*}): the camera advances instead, and for large angles the points pass behind it. The mean gives a pure rotation for every angle below π; at exactly π it stops, in a singular configuration.

Straight lines in the image are not a sensible 3-D motion: IBVS controls the image, not the camera path.

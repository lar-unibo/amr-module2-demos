The idea

One camera sees a point along a ray, but not how far along it: the depth is lost. Two cameras see it along two rays, and the point is where the rays meet. In the top view (left), drag the red point: the two dashed rays follow it.

Disparity

In normal stereo the two cameras are identical, with parallel optical axes, and the right one is shifted by the baseline b along x. A point then appears on the same row of both images (the epipolar line), shifted by the disparity d=u_L-u_R (the orange arrow, right). Similar triangles give

Z=\frac{b\,f'}{d},\qquad f'=\frac{f}{\rho_w}

with f' the focal length in pixels.

Try: drag the point up, away from the cameras: the rays become almost parallel and the disparity shrinks like 1/Z. Bring it close: large disparity. Move it sideways: both image points move, the disparity does not change.

Only some depths are possible

The disparity is measured by matching pixels, so it comes in whole pixels (or fractions of a pixel, with sub-pixel matching). Each value of d gives one depth: the camera can only report the depths of the dashed lines in the top view, and the measured point (red ring) jumps from line to line.

The lines are dense near the cameras and sparse far away. One disparity step changes the depth by about

\Delta Z\approx\frac{Z^2}{b\,f'}\,\Delta d,

so the depth error grows like Z^2: about 11 cm at 3 m, more than 1.5 m at 12 m with the default camera.

Try: increase the baseline or the focal length: the lines get denser and the depth more precise. Switch to 1/4 pixel: four times denser.

From pixels to 3-D points

Once the depth is known (from stereo, structured light or time of flight), a pixel (u,v) gives the 3-D point in the camera frame:

\vP_C=Z\,\vK^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\1\end{bmatrix},\quad X=Z\,\frac{u-u_0}{f/\rho_w},\quad Y=Z\,\frac{v-v_0}{f/\rho_h}

The card on the right does this with the depth measured by the stereo pair. The hard part in practice is not this formula but the correspondence problem: finding the same point in both images, which fails on textureless surfaces.

Coordinate frames

A calibrated camera and a 3-D model of the target give, at every frame, the pose of the current camera \mathcal F_c (red) with respect to the object \mathcal F_o. The task is the desired camera \mathcal F_d (blue). Their composition

{}^d\vT_c=({}^o\vT_d)^{-1}\,{}^o\vT_c =\begin{bmatrix}{}^d\vR_c&{}^d\vt_c\\\mathbf 0^{T}&1\end{bmatrix}

must become the identity: {}^d\vt_c\to\mathbf 0 and {}^d\vR_c\to\vI.

Features and control law

The features are the translation and the angle-axis vector of the rotation, \vs=({}^d\vt_c,\ \theta\vm), with \vs^{*}=\mathbf 0. The interaction matrix is block-diagonal, and since \vL_{\theta\vm}^{-1}\theta\vm=\theta\vm:

\nu_c=-\lambda\,{}^d\vR_c^{T}\,{}^d\vt_c,\qquad \omega_c=-\lambda\,\theta\vm.

Both errors decay as e^{-\lambda t} (top-left plot). The card on the right shows {}^d\vt_c, \theta and \vm at the time of the slider: \vm does not change.

A straight line in 3-D, curves in the image

With a perfect pose estimate, the camera follows the straight line to \mathcal F_d (dashed) and rotates about a fixed axis. Nothing controls the image: the points follow curves.

Try: large tilt. The camera path is still straight, but the points leave the image on the way (bottom-right plot above 1). A real system would lose the target, and with it the pose estimate.

Rotations about the optical axis are easy for PBVS: try π rad about z, which defeats IBVS (next demos).
